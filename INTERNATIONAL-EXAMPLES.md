# International Policy Examples — What's Worth Learning From

> A curated reference for the Open Digital Policies project.
> These are real-world enacted policies worth studying, adapting, and citing in model language.
> Last updated: 2026-04-04

---

## How to Use This Document

This is a living reference, not a ranking. Each example is assessed for:
- **What it does well** — the specific provisions or principles worth adopting
- **What it doesn't yet do** — gaps worth addressing in model language
- **Who it could help** — which jurisdictions and communities could most benefit from adopting something similar

Examples are drawn from civil society analysis, EFF, EDRi, FSFE, and academic review — not only from government self-description.

## France — A Global Leader in Digital Environmental Policy

France has enacted the most comprehensive national framework for reducing the environmental footprint of digital technology of any country. Three laws form the core of this framework and each contributes exportable model language.

---

### 🇫🇷 AGEC Law (Anti-Waste and Circular Economy Act, 2020)

**Full name:** Loi relative à la lutte contre le gaspillage et à l'économie circulaire (Law No. 2020-105)
**Enacted:** February 2020
**Covers:** Consumer products, electronics, planned obsolescence, repairability

#### What it does well

**Repairability Index (Indice de réparabilité).** France mandated a scored repairability label — 0 to 10 — for consumer electronics including smartphones, laptops, TVs, and washing machines, in force from January 2021. The score assesses: documentation availability, disassembly ease, availability and pricing of spare parts, and product-specific factors. It is displayed at point of sale. This is the first national mandated repairability scoring system in the world.

**Durability Index (Indice de durabilité).** From 2025, the repairability index is evolving into a broader durability index covering additional criteria (reliability, maintainability). This represents the maturing of the framework from initial launch.

**Spare parts availability mandate.** Under AGEC, manufacturers must make spare parts available for a minimum of 5 years for small digital equipment (phones, tablets, laptops) and 10 years for larger appliances. This directly addresses the "parts unavailability" barrier that makes repair impossible even where skills and intent exist.

**Extended legal guarantee for refurbished products.** Refurbished products sold in France must carry a minimum 2-year guarantee — same as new products — removing the quality-perception barrier to buying refurbished.

**Goal:** Increase the proportion of products that get repaired from 40% to 60% within five years of 2021.

#### What it doesn't yet do

- The repairability index is self-reported by manufacturers; independent verification is limited
- Enforcement against false or inflated scores is underdeveloped
- Does not yet cover agricultural equipment, vehicles, or professional/medical devices

#### What to adopt

The repairability/durability index model — scored, mandatory, displayed at point of sale — is directly exportable to any jurisdiction with consumer product law. It creates market incentives without prohibiting any product design. The scoring methodology is publicly documented and reusable.

*Relevant ODP domain:* [Right to Repair & Interoperability](policies/right-to-repair/)
*Reference:* [AGEC Law overview (Ellen MacArthur Foundation)](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/circular-examples/frances-anti-waste-and-circular-economy-law)

### 🇫🇷 REEN Law (Reduction of the Environmental Footprint of Digital Technology Act, 2021)

**Full name:** Loi visant à réduire l'empreinte environnementale du numérique en France (Law No. 2021-1485)
**Enacted:** November 2021
**Covers:** Data centers, public procurement, local authorities, digital eco-design, e-waste

This is the most comprehensive national law targeting the environmental footprint of digital technology. It is ahead of anything at the EU level and ahead of any other country as of 2026.

#### What it does well

**Mandatory responsible digital strategy for municipalities.** From 2025, all French municipalities with more than 50,000 inhabitants must publish a responsible digital strategy including: inventory of digital assets; environmental footprint assessment; objectives for reduction; and measures taken. This is a powerful local government obligation — it makes digital sustainability a planning responsibility, not just a technology choice.

**Data center energy efficiency tightening.** The REEN law tightened existing environmental requirements for data centers and created a tax incentive (reduced domestic electricity tax) for data centers that meet the stricter efficiency standards. This is a carrot-and-stick approach: higher standards plus financial reward for compliance.

**Waste heat recovery mandate.** Territorial climate-air-energy plans (PCAET) must integrate heat recovery from data centers into regional planning. This is the only national law requiring data center waste heat recovery to be planned at the urban/territorial scale.

**Repairability in public procurement.** From January 2023, public bodies must take repairability and durability indices into account when procuring digital products. From January 2026, sustainability index scores are required considerations. This embeds the AGEC consumer-facing indices into government purchasing — one of the strongest levers for market transformation.

**Digital eco-design reference framework.** The REEN law directed the development of a national digital eco-design reference framework — a set of best practices for sustainable digital product and service design. This is the legislative basis for something analogous to the W3C Web Sustainability Guidelines becoming a government reference standard.

**Awareness and education provisions.** The law includes requirements for digital environmental literacy in education and for awareness-raising in the technology sector — recognising that regulatory change must be accompanied by cultural change.

#### What it doesn't yet do

- Does not yet mandate carbon or water footprint reporting for data centers (only efficiency metrics)
- Municipal strategy requirement is a planning obligation, not a performance requirement — there is no enforcement mechanism if the strategy is weak
- The data center efficiency incentive is opt-in; non-compliance has no direct penalty beyond loss of tax benefit
- No mechanism for community input into municipal digital strategies

#### What to adopt

**The municipal digital strategy obligation** is the most exportable provision. Any local government could adopt a version of this: an inventory of digital assets, a footprint assessment, reduction targets, and annual reporting. It creates accountability without requiring specific technical standards. It is particularly powerful because it applies to government itself, not only to industry.

**The public procurement / repairability index integration** is immediately actionable by any jurisdiction that has or can adopt a repairability scoring system: require that scores be disclosed in procurement, and weight higher scores positively.

**The waste heat recovery planning requirement** is a model for integrating digital infrastructure into urban climate planning — an approach no other jurisdiction has yet adopted at this scale.

*Relevant ODP domains:* [Data Centers](policies/data-centers/), [Web Sustainability](policies/web-sustainability/), [Right to Repair](policies/right-to-repair/)
*References:* [REEN Law summary (Fruggr)](https://www.fruggr.io/en/reen-law-mitigating-the-environmental-impact-of-digital-technologies/); [Overview of French Sustainable IT Regulations](https://www.apl-datacenter.com/en/overview-of-french-sustainable-it-regulations/)

### 🇫🇷 Climate and Resilience Act (2021)

**Enacted:** August 2021
**Relevant provisions:** Digital advertising restrictions; environmental claims ("greenwashing") enforcement; carbon labelling for digital services

The Climate and Resilience Act includes provisions relevant to digital sustainability:
- Prohibits certain forms of digital advertising for high-carbon products
- Strengthens enforcement against environmental claims not backed by evidence ("greenwashing") — relevant to data center "100% renewable" claims
- Creates a framework for carbon labelling of products and services

## Estonia — The World's Most Advanced Digital Government

Estonia's digital government is internationally renowned — but often described in aspirational or technical terms that miss the specific policy and legislative choices that made it possible. These are the provisions worth exporting.

---

### 🇪🇺 EU Single Digital Gateway Regulation — Once-Only Made Law Across 27 Member States

**Legislative basis:** Regulation (EU) 2018/1724
**Once-Only Technical System (OOTS) live:** December 12, 2023
**Covers:** 21 priority administrative procedures fully online, cross-border, once-only across all EU member states

Estonia invented it. The EU made it law for a bloc of 450 million people.

The EU Single Digital Gateway Regulation requires that for 21 key administrative procedures — registering a business, applying for a health insurance card, requesting proof of birth, enrolling in university — citizens do not need to re-submit documents that any EU member state authority already holds. The Once-Only Technical System (OOTS) enables automated cross-border evidence exchange between competent authorities, with user consent.

**What this adds to the Estonia model:**
- It is legally binding across 27 member states — not a best practice but a mandate
- It applies *cross-border*, not just within a national government — the hardest version of the problem
- It is backed by EU infringement proceedings if member states fail to comply
- The technical infrastructure (OOTS) is open and documented — other countries can adopt it

**Where implementation stands (2024–2025):** As of 2023, 12 member states had reached production readiness; 3 had their first live citizen transactions. The challenge is not the technology but the governance: getting individual competent authorities within member states to connect their systems as both data providers and data requesters. This is the political economy problem of interoperability everywhere — the technology works; the institutional inertia does not.

**Harvard Kennedy School assessment (2024):** Belgium, Estonia, and the Netherlands have the most advanced national once-only implementations. 22 of 30 European countries have some national legislation on data sharing for this purpose. The limiting factor is almost always lack of a legal mandate for *specific agencies* to share specific data — general authority exists but departmental autonomy remains.

**What to adopt from the EU model:**
> "No person shall be required to submit to a public authority any document, data, or information that is already lawfully held by another public authority, where such information can be securely retrieved with the person's consent. Public authorities shall be legally required to query existing data before requesting information from individuals."

This is the once-only principle as positive model language. The Harvard policy brief identifies this as one of the highest-leverage digital government reforms available to any jurisdiction — it reduces burden, forces data minimisation, and requires agencies to actually share infrastructure.

*Relevant ODP domain:* [Digital Sovereignty](policies/digital-sovereignty/), [Open Source in Government](policies/open-source/)
*References:* [EU Single Digital Gateway – EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/the-single-digital-gateway.html); [OOTS implementation (Digdir Norway)](https://www.digdir.no/internasjonalt-arbeid/project-testing-single-digital-gateway-once-only-technical-system/5042); [Harvard KSG: Deploying the Once-Only Policy (2024)](https://ash.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/deploying-once-only-policy.pdf)

### 🇪🇪 The Once-Only Principle (Embedded in Law Since 2007)

**Legislative basis:** Public Information Act (2000, amended); Digital Signatures Act (2000)
**Key provision:** Since 2007, Estonia's Public Information Act **prohibits** the creation of separate databases to collect the same data that already exists in another government database.

#### What it does

The once-only principle means that a citizen provides information to government once — and government agencies are legally required to share and reuse that information internally rather than asking for it again. When you register a change of address, every government agency that needs your address gets it automatically. You do not fill out 15 separate forms.

This is not just a usability improvement — it is a structural constraint on government data collection. Government cannot collect data it doesn't need because it cannot have a separate database for data that already exists elsewhere. The prohibition is the policy mechanism; the convenience is the user experience result.

#### The X-Road infrastructure

The once-only principle is made technically possible by X-Road — Estonia's open source data exchange layer, built in 2001, that allows different government databases to talk to each other securely. Key features:

- **Decentralised.** No central mega-database. Each agency maintains its own database; X-Road creates the secure exchange layer between them.
- **Logged and auditable.** Every data query is logged. Citizens can see — in their personal portal — which government agencies have accessed which of their data and when. This is transparency by design.
- **Open source.** X-Road is maintained as open source by the Nordic Institute for Interoperability Solutions (NIIS), a joint Estonian-Finnish institution. It has been adopted by over 20 countries.
- **Citizen control.** Citizens can restrict access to some of their data, and can query the access log to identify any unexpected access.

#### What the once-only principle gets right that most digital government doesn't

Most "digital transformation" in government means taking a paper form and putting it on a website. Estonia's model means fundamentally redesigning the information relationship between citizen and state: the citizen is the source of truth once; government then stewards that data with legal obligations of necessity, accuracy, and transparency.

This inverts the usual dynamic where government collects as much data as possible. It creates structural data minimisation — one of the core GDPR principles — not through compliance checklists, but through architecture and law.

#### What it doesn't do

- Estonia's model has been built over 30 years by a small, technically capable, high-trust government. The political preconditions (strong data protection culture, public trust in government) are not universal.
- X-Road works within a national context. Cross-border interoperability — even between Estonia and Finland, which collaborated on X-Road — requires significant additional governance.
- The system relies on a national digital identity (ID card with cryptographic chip). Jurisdictions without a trusted national ID face a harder path to the same result.
- The citizen transparency provisions (access log) are not fully replicated in most adoptions of X-Road — some countries adopt the infrastructure without the accountability layer.

#### What to adopt

**The prohibition on redundant data collection** is directly exportable as model language: *"No government body shall create or maintain a database for the purpose of collecting information that is already held in another government database, except where such separation is required for security or privacy protection purposes, documented and approved by [designated authority]."*

**The access log as a citizen right** is the strongest accountability provision: citizens can see who accessed what data when, without having to ask. This transforms data transparency from a compliance exercise into an operational right.

**X-Road as open source public infrastructure.** The fact that X-Road is open source and maintained by a multilateral public institution (NIIS) is a model for digital public goods — shared infrastructure that no single vendor owns.

## EU Laws Worth Adopting Beyond Europe

*(Cross-reference with EU-LAWS-ANALYSIS.md for full assessment)*

| Law | Best provision to export | ODP domain |
|---|---|---|
| **GDPR** | Rights architecture: access, erasure, portability, privacy by design | Smart Cities, AI |
| **EU AI Act** | Absolute prohibitions: biometric mass surveillance, social scoring, emotion recognition at work | AI Adoption |
| **Digital Markets Act** | Interoperability by design; data portability APIs; no self-preferencing | Digital Sovereignty, Right to Repair |
| **EU Right to Repair Directive** | Manufacturer repair obligation; spare parts access; no repair-blocking software conditions | Right to Repair |
| **EU Cyber Resilience Act** | Lifetime security support; vulnerability disclosure | Data Centers, Open Source |
| **Web Accessibility Directive + EAA** | WCAG mandate for public sector; accessibility statements; procurement requirements | Accessibility |
| **EU Code of Conduct for Data Centres** | PUE/WUE standards; renewable energy commitment; waste heat recovery | Data Centers |

## Other Examples Worth Tracking

### 🇨🇦 Canada Bill C-244 — Right to Repair Copyright Exception (2024)
First national law to create a copyright exception for repair. Directly addressable for any jurisdiction using copyright law as a repair barrier. See [Right to Repair model](policies/right-to-repair/).

### 🇩🇪 Germany Energy Efficiency Act (EnEfG, 2023)
Requires data centers to use 50% renewable energy (2024) rising to 100% (2027); PUE ≤ 1.2 for new data centers from 2026; waste heat recovery required where feasible. The strongest enacted national data center sustainability standard. See [Data Centers model](policies/data-centers/).

### 🇮🇪 Ireland Data Centre Grid Policy (2022)
First national policy to assess data center grid impact as a condition of planning permission. See [Data Centers model](policies/data-centers/).

### 🇳🇱 Amsterdam Data Centre Moratorium (2019–2022)
First major municipal-level assertion of community control over data center siting. See [Data Centers model](policies/data-centers/).

### 🇬🇧 UK Children's Code (Age-Appropriate Design Code, 2020)
The most comprehensive enacted framework for age-appropriate design in digital products. Covers default privacy settings, data minimisation, and prohibition on nudge techniques targeting children. See planned [Children & Technology model](policies/children-technology/).

### 🇰🇷 South Korea — AI Ethics and Accountability Framework (2020–2024)
Korea has enacted a National AI Ethics Charter and begun developing legally binding accountability requirements for AI in public services. Often overlooked in English-language policy analysis; an important non-European reference for AI governance.

### 🇧🇷 Brazil — LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, 2018)
Brazil's GDPR-equivalent, in force 2020. The most significant privacy law in Latin America and a model for the region. Enforcement has been uneven but the rights architecture is strong.

### 🇰🇪 Kenya — Data Protection Act (2019)
The most comprehensive privacy law in Sub-Saharan Africa. Based on GDPR principles. Important for a repository that aims to be global, not just European.

## What This Repository Still Needs

Examples from the following regions are critically underrepresented:

- **Southeast Asia** — digital governance frameworks from Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand
- **Sub-Saharan Africa** — beyond Kenya, the e-waste policies emerging in Nigeria, Ghana, Rwanda
- **Latin America** — Brazil's LGPD implementation experience; Colombia, Mexico, Argentina frameworks
- **Pacific** — Pacific Island nations facing unique digital sovereignty challenges with infrastructure controlled by US/Australian cable operators

If you have expertise in any of these regions, contributions to this document and to the model policy examples sections are especially welcome.
